\begin{abstract}
\noindent What happens to local democracy when weak states share territorial authority with armed groups? We study metropolitan Rio de Janeiro, where paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking factions control neighborhoods but differ in their relationship with the state. We match annual faction territory maps to polling stations across five municipal elections and estimate staggered difference-in-differences effects relative to not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type. Militia territorial entry is associated with lower mayoral competition. Vote concentration (HHI) rises by 0.039 and the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points; the effective number of candidates falls by 0.347, an arithmetic translation of the HHI movement under the Laakso-Taagepera identity $\text{ENC}\equiv 1/\text{HHI}$. Turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. Mechanism estimates show that votes shift toward elected, locally leading, and better-financed mayoral candidates, with vote-weighted spending per vote falling. Drug-trafficking faction expansion produces no comparable estimates under the same design. Neither faction type affects city-council races. The asymmetry is consistent with electoral capture being concentrated where criminal governance is embedded in local state authority, the institutional channel documented separately by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}.

\noindent\textit{JEL}: D72, D74, K42, O17 \quad \textit{Keywords}: electoral competition, militias, organized crime, difference-in-differences, staggered treatment, Brazil
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==========================================================================

Weak states rarely lose territorial authority in a uniform way. They often share it with armed organizations whose ties to public institutions range from confrontation to collusion \citep{acemoglu2020weak, blattman2025gangrule}. Organized crime can then enter elections by controlling votes, using violence around campaigns, or exchanging armed support for protection and economic rents \citep{defeo2017mafia, alesina2019organized, acemoglu2013monopoly}. We ask whether the institutional form of criminal governance determines when territorial control becomes electoral capture. Local elections provide a sharp test because they allocate contracts, land-use decisions, regulatory enforcement, and public-security priorities where state authority is contested.

Metropolitan Rio de Janeiro places this contrast inside one urban economy. Paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking factions control neighborhoods where millions of voters live \citep{monteiro2023enterprises}. Drug-trafficking factions govern through armed presence and confrontation with public security forces \citep{arias2006drugs}. Militias grew out of networks of current and former police, firefighters, and military personnel. They extract rents from service monopolies in gas, internet, transit, and real estate. They also participate in elections through voter mobilization, campaign finance, and political protection networks \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. These rents depend on municipal authority over enforcement, licensing, land use, contracts, and appointments. The hypothesis we take to the data is that territorial control should compress electoral competition when armed governance is embedded in local state authority, while it should be weaker when armed governance is mainly confrontational.

We match annual georeferenced territory maps from Fogo Cruzado to polling-station returns from five municipal elections in the 20 municipalities of metropolitan Rio de Janeiro. Identification comes from the staggered timing of territorial entry. As faction polygons grow across election cycles, previously untreated polling stations enter faction territory at different times. We estimate heterogeneity-robust average treatment effects \citep{callaway2021did} separately by faction type. Newly treated stations are compared with not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type. The comparison removes the fixed differences between inside-territory and outside-any-territory stations that would confound a never-treated design. Identification rests on the parallel-trends assumption stated in equation~\eqref{eq:cpt} below; we discuss how reverse causality, geographic selection, and the COVID-19 cohort qualify a strict causal reading.

Under the preferred not-yet-treated specification, militia territorial entry is associated with lower mayoral competition: vote concentration (HHI) rises by 0.039, the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points, and turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. The effective number of candidates falls by 0.347, an arithmetic translation of the HHI movement under the Laakso-Taagepera identity $\text{ENC}\equiv 1/\text{HHI}$ rather than independent corroboration. Relative to pre-treatment militia baselines, these estimates imply an 11 percent increase in concentration and a 25 percent wider mayoral margin. Drug-trafficking faction expansion produces no detectable estimates on the same outcomes under the same design. Neither faction type moves open-list proportional city-council races, which is consistent with armed groups concentrating political resources where executive rents are largest and candidate coordination is easiest.

We estimate the electoral side of armed territorial control at the polling-station level. The design uses Fogo Cruzado annual territory polygons and compares militia and drug-trafficking faction expansion inside the same metropolitan economy. The comparison separates territorial control from the institutional form of criminal governance. It complements evidence from Rio that criminal governance can affect elections through candidate gatekeeping and voter corralling \citep{trudeau2025criminal}. The results also speak to the elections-to-militia channel documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}, who show that elected officials protect militia expansion. We document estimates consistent with the reverse channel, in which militia territorial entry coincides with mayoral competition compression. We do not estimate both directions inside this paper: a feedback interpretation requires combining our estimates with the institutional channel in \citet{pantaleao2025elections}. Read together, the two sets of estimates describe complementary, mutually reinforcing channels rather than a single jointly estimated equilibrium \citep{lessing2021, acemoglu2020weak, acemoglu2013monopoly}.

The institutional contrast in metropolitan Rio motivates the empirical design that follows.

\section{Institutional Context}\label{sec:context}
%==========================================================================

Rio de Janeiro's armed factions emerged in the 1980s with the expansion of cocaine trafficking through the city's \emph{favelas} \citep{arias2006drugs}. The Comando Vermelho (CV) was the first major drug-trafficking faction, followed by rivals Terceiro Comando Puro (TCP) and Amigos dos Amigos (ADA). These factions control territory through armed force, collecting ``taxes'' from residents and businesses and regulating daily life within their domains \citep{lessing2017logics}. Throughout the paper we refer to these groups as \emph{drug-trafficking factions} and use the shorthand ``drug'' only in tables and figures where space is constrained.

Militias\footnote{Paramilitary groups composed primarily of active and retired police, firefighters, and military personnel} expanded rapidly from the early 2000s, particularly in Rio's West Zone \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. Unlike drug-trafficking factions, militias monopolize the provision of cooking gas, internet, public transit, and real estate within their territories. By 2024, militia-controlled territories covered a larger area than drug-trafficking factions in the metropolitan region.

The two groups occupy different positions in criminal-governance theory. \citet{magaloni2020killing} classify regimes by territorial monopoly, the relationship with the state, and treatment of the community. Rio's drug-trafficking factions usually fit \emph{Insurgent} or \emph{Bandit} regimes, where armed groups confront state forces. Militias fit \emph{Symbiotic} or \emph{Predatory} regimes, where criminal rule is tied to collusion. In \citet{blattman2025gangrule}, state rule and criminal rule can complement each other when armed groups hold rents worth protecting. Militias are an extreme case because the police-militia overlap brings criminal governance into the state's own personnel networks. In Brazil, politicians embedded in law-enforcement networks can distort local security provision even when formal municipal authority over policing is limited \citep{novaes2023laworder}. \citet{monteiro2023enterprises} document the economic side of the militia-drug contrast. Militias dominate local provision of cooking gas, cable TV, internet, water, electricity, and real estate. Drug-trafficking factions remain more concentrated in drug retail. Broader economic stakes raise the value of local political control.

The expected electoral effect should therefore vary by faction type. Drug-trafficking factions operate in a confrontational relationship with the state and have narrower economic footprints. That limits their incentive and institutional capacity to convert territorial control into vote shares. Militias operate through collusive relationships and hold rents that depend on local-state cooperation. They have stronger reasons to translate territory into electoral capture \citep{trejo2020votes, snyder2009protection, pantaleao2025elections}. Rio evidence shows that criminal groups can broker votes through gatekeeping and corralling \citep{trudeau2025criminal}. The prediction here concerns which form of territorial expansion should measurably compress competition under the same design. Militia influence should be visible as territory is held across election cycles. Drug-trafficking faction expansion should produce a weaker electoral signature. \section{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:data}
%==========================================================================

The analysis is at the polling-station-by-election-year level. We construct treatment from annual faction territory maps produced by the Fogo Cruzado Institute, a Rio civil-society observatory that georeferences control polygons for paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking factions in metropolitan Rio de Janeiro \citep{fogocruzado2024}. The maps combine official gazettes, local press coverage, and triangulated incident reports on armed confrontation. Annual polygons cover 2007 to 2024. Appendix~\ref{app:measurement} documents treatment construction and validation. The measure captures the timing of \textit{de facto} territorial control as recorded by Fogo Cruzado. It is not an administrative or legal measure. Detection may be more systematic for militia territory, which often operates through visible service monopolies, than for drug-trafficking territory, which is more fluid and covert. We return to this asymmetry in the discussion because it implies that classical measurement error attenuates drug-trafficking estimates more than militia estimates, so a literal comparison of point estimates understates the militia-drug gap rather than overstating it.

Electoral competition outcomes are constructed from polling-station-level returns published by Brazil's Superior Electoral Tribunal \citep{tse2024} for five municipal elections in 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024. For each polling station, election, and office (\emph{prefeito} or \emph{vereador}), we compute the Herfindahl-Hirschman index of candidate vote shares (HHI) and the margin of victory between the first- and second-place candidates. We also report the effective number of candidates (ENC, following \citealt{laakso1979}). HHI and ENC are not independent outcomes: by the Laakso-Taagepera definition $\text{ENC}\equiv 1/\text{HHI}$, so any movement in HHI maps mechanically into a movement in ENC, and the two estimates carry the same information up to a non-linear monotone transformation. We report ENC alongside HHI because the two scales speak to different audiences: HHI is the natural concentration metric in the political-economy literature, while ENC translates the same variation into an interpretable count of effective candidates. We treat HHI as the primary concentration outcome and the margin of victory as a separate, non-redundant measure of competition. We also obtain the number of eligible voters at each station-election from TSE, which we use as analytic weights and as a baseline demographic proxy.

The geocoded master roster contains 4,180 polling stations across five elections. Following \citet{monteiro2023enterprises}, we classify treated stations by stable faction type during the treated period. The roster contains 401 stable militia stations, 246 stable drug-trafficking stations, 65 stations that switch between militia and drug-trafficking control, and 3,468 stations that never fall inside any annual faction territory from 2007 to 2024. The main treated estimation sample excludes switchers and requires valid electoral outcomes and eligible-voter weights. It contains 398 militia stations and 240 drug-trafficking stations in mayoral races. Treatment cohorts are defined by the first election year in which a station falls inside the faction's territory. Stations inside territory since the first map year in 2007 are assigned $g = 2008$. The staggered timing of entry provides the identifying variation.

The outcomes capture different margins of electoral competition. HHI measures the concentration of vote shares across candidates and rises when votes concentrate on one candidate. ENC expresses the same distribution in units of equivalent competing candidates and is the reciprocal of HHI. The margin of victory rises when the winner's lead widens and carries information that is not deducible from HHI. These outcomes help distinguish channels. Coercive mobilization toward a preferred candidate should raise HHI and widen the margin. Candidate deterrence should widen the margin with a smaller direct effect on HHI. A common movement in HHI and the margin is consistent with both channels operating through militia control.

We exploit the staggered timing of faction territorial entry in a difference-in-differences framework. Let $G_i \in \{2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024, \infty\}$ denote the first election year at which polling station $i$ falls inside a faction territory of a given type, with $G_i = \infty$ for never-treated stations. We restrict the estimation sample to stations with stable faction classification, so treatment is irreversible \emph{by construction} in this sample. Excluded switchers (40 drug $\rightarrow$ militia and 25 militia $\rightarrow$ drug, 9.1\% of ever-treated stations) experience two treatment events and are dropped to keep the within-faction-type ATT well defined. The exclusion narrows external validity to areas of stable faction control rather than contested boundaries.

The design estimates group-time average treatment effects on the treated,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:att_gt}
ATT(g,t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(g) - Y_t(\infty) \mid G_i = g\big],
\end{equation}
where $Y_t(g)$ is the potential outcome at election $t$ under territorial entry in cohort $g$, and $Y_t(\infty)$ is the potential outcome without treatment. We estimate $ATT(g,t)$ using the doubly robust estimator of \citet{callaway2021did}. The estimator combines inverse probability weighting with outcome regression and is consistent if either the propensity score or the outcome model is correctly specified. Observations are weighted by the number of eligible voters at each station-election, so estimated ATTs represent effects on the voter population rather than on the polling-station population. These populations differ in dense urban voting locations.

The identifying assumption is conditional parallel trends. Conditional on pre-treatment covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$, the expected change in untreated potential outcomes is the same for group-$g$ stations and the comparison group. Using not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type as controls,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cpt}
\mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(\infty) - Y_{t-1}(\infty) \mid \mathbf{X}_i,\, G_i = g\big]
  \;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(\infty) - Y_{t-1}(\infty) \mid \mathbf{X}_i,\, G_i > t\big]
\end{equation}
for all $t \geq g$. In the main specification we set $\mathbf{X}_i = \emptyset$, imposing unconditional parallel trends within each faction-type pool. Conditioning is tightly constrained by the polling-station level of analysis. Stations are administrative units rather than survey units, and routinely observed station-level covariates are limited to the number of eligible voters used as weights, the parent municipality, and the station's own voting record.

Faction territorial entry may be endogenous to polling-station electoral dynamics. Armed groups may expand into neighborhoods where a politically aligned candidate is vulnerable, or where support for that candidate is already consolidating. The reverse-causality channel documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}, in which elected officials protect militia expansion, is a direct concern. A second concern is geographic contagion. Militia expansion often follows existing territorial boundaries that correlate with persistent features of the Rio metropolitan map, including the West Zone and the Baixada Fluminense. A third concern is the 2020 cohort, which overlaps with the COVID-19 shock to campaigns and turnout. Together these threats mean we cannot read the design as a clean randomized experiment, and we adopt a moderated empirical claim throughout: estimates are reported as conditional differences between entering and not-yet-entering militia stations of the same faction type, not as exogenous treatment effects.

Rio's Pacifying Police Units (UPPs), rolled out from 2008 to 2014, weaken the concern that militia entry simply tracks station-level electoral dynamics. UPPs occupied 38 favelas and displaced incumbent drug-trafficking factions. Their rollout followed logistical and political criteria tied to the 2014 World Cup and 2016 Olympics, rather than station-level electoral dynamics \citep{ferraz2023upp, magaloni2020killing}. The criminal-governance literature indicates that militias expanded into territorial vacuums created by drug-trafficking-faction displacement from UPPs and later state crackdowns \citep{pantaleao2025elections, monteiro2023enterprises}. If the militia-entry cohorts from 2012 to 2020 partly reflect these state-induced displacement shocks, the timing is not driven only by local political conditions. We do not formalize this argument as an instrumental-variables strategy and read the estimates as effects of militia territorial entry conditional on the state-policy component of entry timing rather than as a sharp causal identification.

The within-faction-type NYT comparison gives treated stations a counterfactual drawn from the same institutional mechanism. It removes selection into faction type and reduces concerns about geographic contagion, since treated and not-yet-treated stations come from the same expansion process. Event-study paths address the COVID concern. If the 2020 cohort drove the aggregate estimates, the path would show a discontinuity at $e=0$ driven by post-2020 cells. The event-study paths below are more stable.

NYT controls of the same faction type are the preferred comparison group. For a militia-treated station in cohort $g$, controls are militia-classified stations with $G_i > t$. The drug-trafficking-faction design is analogous. These controls come from the same institutional mechanism as treated units, so the comparison isolates timing from faction-type selection. Appendix~\ref{app:nt} reports a broader outside-stable-treatment benchmark. In mayoral races, that benchmark uses 3,791 valid polling stations that are not in the stable militia or stable drug-trafficking treated samples. It differs from the 3,468 true never-treated stations in the geocoded roster because it is defined on the electoral panel and excludes only stable treated units. We use NYT in the main text.

Because treatment is defined on annual territory maps while elections occur every four years, the first post-treatment observation may reflect only partial within-cycle exposure for stations that entered territory late in the four-year cycle. If militia territory expanded to include a station in 2018, that station's first post-treatment election is 2020, with only two years of exposure. This feature may attenuate short-run estimates. The event-time-zero estimate should therefore be interpreted as a partial-exposure estimate rather than as the effect of a full municipal cycle under faction control.

Dynamic specifications measure event time in elections relative to cohort entry. The universal base period is $e = -1$, so coefficients are deviations from the election immediately before treatment. Cohorts first observed as treated in 2008 have no pre-treatment municipal election in the panel. They are included in treated-station counts, but they do not contribute to $ATT(g,t)$ cells that require the universal pre-treatment base period. We summarize dynamics using the aggregation in \citet{callaway2021did}. It averages $ATT(g,t)$ cells by event time $e$ and then averages the post-treatment path with weights proportional to the number of cohorts that reach each horizon. The figures report $e \in \{-12, -8, -4, 0, +4\}$ years relative to cohort entry. Standard errors are clustered at the polling-station level using the influence-function multiplier bootstrap in the \texttt{did} R package. Pre-trends are assessed with a joint Wald test on all event-study coefficients for $e < 0$.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
%==========================================================================

Pre-treatment competition outcomes differ sharply by office. In mayoral races, pre-treatment militia stations have an average HHI of 0.350, an effective number of candidates of 3.43, a margin of victory of 0.21, and turnout of 0.52. Drug-treated and broad-control stations are broadly similar on these dimensions. In city-council races, open-list proportional representation produces a much less concentrated vote distribution. ENC averages around 30 candidates, while HHI and the margin of victory are much smaller than in mayoral races. Table~\ref{tab:sumstats} also reports the mechanism baselines used below: candidate-selection outcomes, campaign-finance outcomes in 2024 reais, and high-spending vote shares. These baselines calibrate the treatment effects below.

\subsection{Mayoral Competition}\label{sec:prefeito_results}

Militia territorial entry is associated with lower competition in mayoral races. Under the preferred NYT specification in Table~\ref{tab:csdid_main}, vote concentration (HHI) rises by 0.039 and the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points; ENC falls by 0.347, the arithmetic translation of the HHI movement. Turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. All estimates are statistically significant at the one percent level and pass the joint Wald pre-trends test.\footnote{We also apply the relative-magnitude sensitivity framework of \citet{rambachan2023honest}. The militia and \emph{prefeito} outcome estimates remain robust to plausible post-treatment deviations from parallel trends. Appendix~\ref{app:honestdid} reports the full sensitivity tables, robust confidence sets, and breakdown values $\overline{M}$.} Event-study estimates have flat pre-treatment coefficients and post-treatment estimates that appear at the treatment election. We address the dynamics in detail below: HHI and ENC attenuate by approximately one-half by the next cycle, while the margin-of-victory estimate is essentially stable. Drug-trafficking faction expansion does not produce detectable estimates on mayoral competition under the same design.

Appendix~\ref{app:mde} formalizes this contrast. Equality tests reject equal militia and drug-trafficking-faction estimates for HHI, ENC, and the margin of victory in mayoral races. Because $\text{ENC}\equiv 1/\text{HHI}$, the HHI and ENC equality tests are not independent pieces of evidence; they are the same test reported on two scales. The corresponding drug-trafficking-faction minimum detectable effects at 80 percent power are 0.036 HHI points, 0.384 effective candidates, and 5.6 percentage points in the margin. We read the drug-trafficking estimates as evidence that the design does not detect comparable electoral compression for drug-trafficking factions in metropolitan Rio over this window. They do not establish a general absence of electoral influence.

The estimates are meaningful relative to pre-treatment militia baselines. The HHI coefficient of $+$0.039 is about 11 percent of the baseline of 0.350; the equivalent ENC coefficient of $-$0.347 is the same movement on the reciprocal scale, about a 10 percent change relative to 3.43 effective candidates. The margin coefficient of $+$0.052 widens the baseline margin by about 25 percent. The turnout coefficient of $+$0.018 is a 3.4 percent increase relative to a baseline of 0.52. Militia entry shifts mayoral outcomes within one election cycle. The dynamic decomposition below shows that the largest concentration estimates appear at entry and partly fade by the next cycle, while the margin estimate remains roughly stable.

Turnout helps separate mobilization from suppression. Higher militia turnout combined with higher concentration and a wider margin is consistent with either mobilization toward a single candidate or selective demobilization that leaves the militia-aligned base relatively over-represented; it is harder to reconcile with broad suppression of all voters. In machine-politics models, rewards often buy turnout because participation is easier to monitor than vote choice \citep{nichter2008turnout}, and brokers can target voters using local knowledge and reciprocal obligations even under the secret ballot \citep{finan2012reciprocity}. The combined pattern leans toward a mobilization channel, but our station-level estimates do not separately identify individual-level vote choice.

\subsection{Mayoral Mechanisms}\label{sec:mechanism_results}

The turnout estimate is more informative when decomposed by vote type. Table~\ref{tab:turnout_decomp} shows that militia expansion raises valid votes for the within-station leading candidate by 0.0265 per eligible voter, while valid votes for all other candidates fall by 0.0101. Blank and null votes do not change detectably. The same decomposition shows no comparable drug-trafficking-faction pattern. The within-station leader is defined \emph{ex post} as the candidate with the highest valid-vote share in the polling-station race, so a finding that ``additional turnout goes to the local leader'' is partly arithmetic: any concentration of new votes on a single candidate makes that candidate the leader. Exposure-duration estimates separate effects at entry from effects four years later. Table~\ref{tab:duration} reports the dynamic estimates at $e=0$ and $e=+4$ alongside the difference. The pattern is one of \emph{partial attenuation}, not stable persistence. The HHI estimate falls from $+0.079$ at entry to $+0.039$ one cycle later, and the ENC estimate moves from $-0.701$ to $-0.347$; both differences are statistically significant and reflect the same underlying movement (HHI and ENC are reciprocals). The margin-of-victory estimate is essentially unchanged across cycles ($+0.078$ vs.\ $+0.077$). The turnout estimate is concentrated in the entry election ($+0.061$) and is statistically indistinguishable from zero one cycle later ($-0.008$); the change is significant. The dynamically aggregated ATT in Table~\ref{tab:csdid_main} averages these horizons, so for HHI, ENC, and turnout it understates the entry-election effect and overstates the one-cycle-later effect.

This pattern is consistent with rapid vote concentration at entry that partially fades as the political situation settles, with a more durable effect on the winning margin and a one-shot effect on participation. Two interpretations are compatible with the data: militia-driven coordination at entry that no longer requires sustained mobilization once a preferred candidate is established; or cohort heterogeneity, with later cohorts reaching $e=+4$ contributing relatively smaller estimates. We do not separately identify the two channels here. We avoid describing the effects as ``persistent'' below; the more accurate characterization is ``present at entry and attenuating, except for the margin of victory.''

Candidate-level and campaign-finance mechanisms point in the same direction. This is the margin on which the organized-crime and machine-politics literatures would expect territorial control to appear. Armed groups can exchange protection, coercion, or market access for political support \citep{dalbo2006plata, defeo2017mafia, acemoglu2013monopoly, alesina2019organized}. In Rio, the same logic is especially plausible for militias because their economic rents depend on state tolerance and local regulatory decisions \citep{monteiro2023enterprises, pantaleao2025elections}, and recent evidence shows that criminal governance can shape elections through candidate gatekeeping and voter corralling \citep{trudeau2025criminal}. The turnout estimate also fits a brokered-mobilization channel: clientelistic brokers often buy or pressure turnout because participation is easier to monitor than vote choice, while local networks help target voters despite the secret ballot \citep{nichter2008turnout, finan2012reciprocity}.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms_prefeito} shows that militia entry is associated with a higher share of votes for candidates who ultimately win the mayoral race, a higher vote share for the locally top candidate and the top three candidates, a lower number of candidates with at least one percentage point of local votes, and a higher share of votes for candidates whose declared occupation is elected office or public administration. Votes also shift toward candidates with larger real campaign expenses and revenues, measured in 2024 reais. At the same time, real vote-weighted expenses per vote fall. Two interpretations of this last estimate are compatible with the data, and the design does not separate them. The first is a productivity reading: territorial assistance makes a real spent on the supported candidate go further, so the cost of producing a vote declines. The second is a compositional reading: militia entry raises the vote share of front-runners (also captured by the top-candidate and elected-share estimates) and front-runners mechanically have more votes per real of declared expense than fringe candidates, so the average expense-per-vote drops without any change in fundamental productivity. We report the negative expense-per-vote estimate as descriptive evidence of where votes shift along the spending distribution and avoid claiming a productivity effect on its own. Drug-trafficking-faction estimates are weaker and mostly indistinguishable from zero, consistent with confrontational criminal governance having weaker access to the political-protection channel.

\subsection{City-Council Competition and Mechanisms}\label{sec:vereador_results}

City-council races do not show the same compression. Under the preferred NYT specification in Table~\ref{tab:csdid_vereador}, none of the three office-specific competition measures moves significantly for either faction type. The militia estimates for HHI ($+0.001$), ENC ($-0.072$, the reciprocal-scale equivalent), and the margin of victory ($+0.003$) are small relative to their baselines and to the mayoral estimates. Relative to militia city-council baselines, they imply a 3.1 percent rise in HHI and a 3.9 percent wider margin. The corresponding mayoral estimates are 11 percent and 25 percent. Drug-trafficking-faction estimates are also small and insignificant. The NYT pre-trends tests do not reject, so the null is not driven by an evident failure of the within-faction-type design.

The office contrast is consistent with Brazil's electoral institutions. Mayoral races are single-seat executive contests, so an armed group can concentrate support on one candidate and, if successful, obtain access to appointments, contracts, inspection priorities, and other municipal levers that matter for illicit and informal markets \citep{ferraz2011electoral, monteiro2023enterprises}. City-council races are open-list proportional contests with many viable candidates and strong personal-vote incentives \citep{carey1995personal, novaes2018brokers}. In that setting, brokers can deliver modular vote blocs to individual politicians without generating visible aggregate compression in the station-level candidate distribution. We use this institutional contrast as the organizing logic for the rest of the paper rather than re-stating it in the discussion section.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================================

Militia territorial entry is associated with lower mayoral competition along the concentration and margin-of-victory dimensions. Relative to pre-treatment militia baselines, HHI rises by 11 percent and the margin widens by 25 percent; the corresponding ENC change is the same movement on the reciprocal scale. Turnout also rises. The combination points away from pure turnout suppression and is more consistent with mobilization or coerced turnout that benefits a single candidate, although our station-level estimates do not separately identify individual vote choice. The mechanism estimates sharpen this reading: militia entry shifts votes toward eventual winners, locally top candidates, and candidates with larger declared campaign budgets. The estimated drop in vote-weighted expense per vote is consistent with either a productivity gain from territorial assistance or a compositional shift toward front-runners who already have low expense per vote; the two are not separately identified here.

Militias occupy an institutional position that can sustain this pattern. They operate through entangled relationships with the state \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. Militias can deliver concentrated electoral support to allied politicians, who can in turn shield militia operations through bureaucratic appointments and law-enforcement priorities. Similar exchanges between armed vote delivery and protection or economic benefits appear in evidence from Sicily and Colombia \citep{defeo2017mafia, acemoglu2013monopoly}. The exchange can combine inducements, threats, and protection, as in formal models of political influence through bribes and punishment \citep{dalbo2006plata}. The design captures the electoral side of such an exchange as territorial control enters; the institutional side is documented separately by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}, and we read the two pieces of evidence as complementary rather than as a feedback loop estimated jointly here. Drug-trafficking faction expansion produces no detectable estimates on any outcome, including turnout. The null is consistent with drug-trafficking factions maintaining control through armed presence and confrontation with the state, with weaker channels of direct political engagement \citep{arias2006drugs, barnes2022territorial}. It is also consistent with the measurement-asymmetry caveat noted in the data section: drug-trafficking territory is more covertly governed and likely measured with more classical error, which attenuates drug-trafficking estimates toward zero. We therefore read the drug-trafficking null as a lower bound on a real difference between the two faction types, not as proof that drug-trafficking factions are electorally inert.

The militia-drug contrast also fits the semi-strong-state framework in \citet{blattman2025gangrule}. A semi-strong state can police illicit markets through repression, crackdowns, and targeted occupations such as Rio's UPPs. It cannot fully dismantle criminal-governance infrastructure, especially when state personnel are embedded in that infrastructure. In this setting, state rule and criminal rule may be complements. Armed groups with rents to protect may respond to state presence by governing more. Rio militias provide a stark case because the police-militia overlap documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections} blurs the line between state rule and criminal rule at the local security level. Criminal rents can then become state-institutional capture through elections. Drug-trafficking factions sit outside this equilibrium and have less access to the institutional channels needed to translate territory into votes.

The scope is institutional rather than geographic. The pattern is most likely in settings where armed groups are embedded in state personnel, control rents that depend on local public authority, and can bargain with elected officials over protection, appointments, contracts, land-use discretion, or enforcement forbearance. The Rio militia-drug contrast does not imply that one Brazilian organization is uniquely political. It is consistent with criminal territorial control becoming electorally consequential when criminal governance and state authority operate as complements.

%==========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================================

Militia territorial entry is associated with lower mayoral competition in metropolitan Rio de Janeiro. Vote concentration rises and the margin of victory widens; the effective number of candidates falls as a reciprocal restatement of the concentration estimate. These estimates pass pre-trends diagnostics and remain robust to plausible violations of parallel trends. Drug-trafficking faction expansion produces no detectable estimates under the same design. Estimates for open-list proportional \emph{vereador} races are also indistinguishable from zero under the preferred specification.

The asymmetry is consistent with the institutional model of political influence documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}. The estimates are consistent with militias converting territorial control into electoral capital through channels involving voter mobilization, campaign-finance advantage, and political protection networks that build over successive election cycles. Drug-trafficking factions show no detectable temporal estimates on electoral competition in this design, although measurement asymmetry between militia and drug-trafficking territory suggests this null should be read as a lower bound on the militia-drug gap. Interventions aimed only at the physical presence of armed groups may miss the institutional machinery that connects territorial control to political power.

\section{Treatment Measurement and Data Provenance}\label{app:measurement}
%==========================================================================

Fogo Cruzado territory maps classify areas of metropolitan Rio de Janeiro by observed armed-group control. They combine official gazettes, local press coverage, and triangulated incident reports on armed confrontation. We treat the maps as measures of observed \textit{de facto} territorial exposure, not as legal or administrative boundaries. This distinction matters because armed governance is more visible in some contexts than in others. Militia control often operates quasi-openly through service monopolies and territorial regulation. Drug-faction control can be more fluid and covert.

We ran three validation checks before assigning treatment status to polling stations. Adjacent annual map vintages show that 97.2\% of militia-classified polygons persist across consecutive years, consistent with the long-run stability of militia control documented in the institutional literature \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. We also cross-checked faction-type classifications against the stable classification of \citet{monteiro2023enterprises} and retained only stations whose attribution is consistent under the stable-classification rule. Finally, we inspected a random sample of polygons against the underlying incident logs and verified that the recorded incidents match the attributed armed-group type. The treatment measures exposure to observed territorial control under a documented mapping protocol. Residual measurement error is more likely to attenuate drug-trafficking-faction estimates than to generate the militia-prefeito pattern.

\section{Honest-DiD Sensitivity Analysis}\label{app:honestdid}

The main specification uses joint Wald tests on pre-treatment event-time coefficients. A non-rejection is necessary for the design but not sufficient for post-treatment parallel trends. Slope violations that the test has low power to detect could still change the sign or significance of the post-treatment estimate. We apply the \citet{rambachan2023honest} sensitivity framework, which computes the breakdown relative-magnitude value $\overline{M}$. This is the largest multiple of the maximum observed pre-period slope that could be allowed as a post-period linear violation while still concluding that the ATT is distinguishable from zero.

\section{Broad Control Benchmark}\label{app:nt}
%==========================================================================

The complementary broad-control specification uses polling stations outside the stable militia and stable drug-trafficking treated samples as controls. In mayoral races, this benchmark contains 3,791 valid control stations. This is not the arithmetic complement of the 4,180-station geocoded roster. The true never-treated geocoded roster contains 3,468 stations, while the broad-control benchmark is defined on the electoral panel and excludes only stable treated units. In Table~\ref{tab:csdid_main_nt}, all three militia and \emph{prefeito} Wald pre-trends tests reject clean pre-trends. Passing NYT pre-trends and failing broad-control pre-trends indicate that stable treated and broad-control stations follow different political trajectories. That difference motivates the within-faction-type design.

The broad-control estimates for \emph{prefeito} races are directionally similar to the NYT results but larger for militia. This is expected when faction-controlled stations are compared with a broader set of polling stations. For drug-trafficking factions, broad-control estimates for HHI, margin of victory, and turnout are also larger than the NYT nulls. Because the militia and \emph{prefeito} Wald tests reject clean pre-trends, we do not interpret these point estimates as causal.

\section{Sample Construction}\label{app:sample}
%==========================================================================

Two features of the raw data complicate panel construction. TSE voting locations are administrative units that change across election cycles. Polling stations can open, close, move, or be renumbered between municipal elections. Fogo Cruzado faction polygons are also revised annually, so ever-controlled territory is larger than any single year's polygon set. A naive year-by-year intersection would mix real territorial entry with changes in administrative and cartographic records. We anchor the panel to time-invariant voting-location and territory frames.

\subsection{Master voting-location roster}

We start from the TSE voting-location file for each election in 2010 through 2024, the first year in which TSE began publishing geocoded latitude and longitude at the voting-location level.\footnote{TSE voting-location files before 2010 do not carry native coordinates. For the 2008 election we inherit coordinates from the earliest subsequent election in which the station appears, under the assumption that voting-location physical addresses are stable across two cycles unless the station was reconstituted.} For each election, we filter to the 20 metropolitan municipalities using the TSE municipality-code crosswalk, and we deduplicate within the election by the triple $(\text{CD\_MUNICIPIO}, \text{NR\_ZONA}, \text{NR\_LOCAL\_VOTACAO})$, which uniquely identifies a polling station within an election.

We build a master roster from the union of all distinct station triples observed in any election year. This gives 4,654 unique stations observed at some point from 2010 to 2024. For the 4,180 stations with coordinates in at least one year, the canonical location is the most recent valid $(\text{lat}, \text{lon})$ pair in the TSE files. The 474 stations without coordinates are dropped from the spatial analysis. The most-recent-coordinate rule avoids spurious station movement when TSE revises the recorded address of a stable physical location. Visual inspection suggests that these address revisions are the main source of year-to-year coordinate changes.

The master roster is unbalanced. Of the 4,180 geocoded stations, 1,740 (41.6\%) appear with a valid mayoral outcome in all five elections, 173 (4.1\%) in four, 1,024 (24.5\%) in three, 979 (23.4\%) in two, 217 (5.2\%) in one, and 47 (1.1\%) in none. The concentration in the three-election and two-election rows reflects a major TSE redistricting of voting locations between 2016 and 2020. Many legacy stations appear only in the elections from 2008 to 2016, while many new stations enter in 2020. We use the unbalanced-panel option in the \texttt{did} R package, which uses each station's available observations to identify cohort-specific $ATT(g,t)$ cells. This keeps the full sample but creates uneven support across cohort cells. The event-study plots show that no single cohort or horizon is identified from a thin subset.

\subsection{Annual master territory map}

Fogo Cruzado polygons are published annually and revised as territorial disputes unfold. A station inside a drug-trafficking-faction polygon in 2010 may be reclassified as militia-controlled in 2018 after a takeover. It may also leave the controlled-polygon set in a year when Fogo Cruzado records a reduction in active armed-group presence. Intersecting each election's voting locations with only that year's polygon map would define treatment against a shifting baseline and mix territorial entry with cartographic revision.

We construct an annual master territory map as the union of all 18 annual control polygons published between 2007 and 2024, computed in UTM 23S projection (EPSG:31983) for metric accuracy. Each voting location receives two measures. The year-specific inside-polygon indicator builds the treatment cohort $G_i$. For each station-year pair, we record whether the station falls inside that year's control polygon and the dominant faction type. The second measure is distance to the ever-controlled envelope, a time-invariant geographic covariate used in robustness checks.

The master map reduces annual polygon noise. Minor boundary revisions between consecutive vintages are subsumed into the union and do not generate spurious entries and exits. Transient gaps, where a station falls inside one map but outside the adjacent vintages, are flagged for manual inspection rather than treated as reversals. In our sample, these cases trace to incident-reporting gaps rather than substantive territorial withdrawal. Master-map distance also gives a stable geographic reference for robustness checks that should not mechanically follow Fogo Cruzado's year-to-year revisions.

\subsection{Faction-type classification and switchers}

Faction type follows the stable classification of \citet{monteiro2023enterprises}, which labels stations by the modal faction attribution across years inside any control polygon. Table~\ref{tab:switchers} reports the distribution for the 712 stations ever classified as inside a polygon. The sample contains 401 stable militia stations, 246 stable drug stations, and 65 switchers. Among switchers, 40 move from drug to militia control and 25 move from militia to drug. Switchers are 9.1\% of ever-treated stations.

We exclude switchers from the estimation sample to isolate faction-specific mechanisms. Keeping them would combine entry into militia control and entry into drug control in a single ATT. It would also weaken the within-faction-type NYT comparison because a station that enters militia territory after a period under drug control experiences two treatment events. Switchers are likely selected on the geographic and political conditions that make an area contested. Dropping them narrows the scope of inference to stable faction control but sharpens identification. A robustness specification that treats switchers as a third treatment arm is feasible but outside the current analysis. The final treated sample requires valid coordinates, a stable faction-type classification, non-missing mayoral outcomes, eligible-voter weights, and location in one of the 20 metropolitan municipalities. These restrictions produce the 398 stable-militia and 240 stable-drug treated stations used in the main mayoral estimates.
