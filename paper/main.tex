\begin{abstract}
\noindent What happens to local democracy when weak states share territorial authority with armed groups? We study metropolitan Rio de Janeiro, where paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking organizations control neighborhoods but differ in their relationship with the state. We match annual faction territory maps to polling stations across five municipal elections and estimate staggered difference-in-differences effects relative to not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type. Militia territorial expansion reduces mayoral competition. Vote concentration rises by 0.039, the effective number of candidates falls by 0.347, the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points, and turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. Drug-faction expansion produces no detectable effects under the same design. Neither faction type affects city-council races. The asymmetry suggests that territorial control becomes electoral capture when criminal governance is embedded in local state authority.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent\textit{JEL}: D72, D74, K42, O17 \quad \textit{Keywords}: electoral competition, militias, organized crime, difference-in-differences, staggered treatment, Brazil
\end{abstract}

%==========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==========================================================================

Weak states rarely lose territorial authority in a uniform way. They share it with armed organizations whose relationship to public institutions ranges from confrontation to collusion \citep{acemoglu2020weak, blattman2023gangrule}. This paper asks whether the institutional form of criminal governance determines when territorial control becomes electoral capture. The question matters for development economics because local elections allocate control over contracts, land-use decisions, regulatory enforcement, and public security priorities in places where state authority is most contested.

Metropolitan Rio de Janeiro is a useful setting because the main armed groups share territory but differ in their links to the state. Paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking factions control neighborhoods where millions of voters live \citep{monteiro2023enterprises}. Drug-trafficking organizations govern through armed presence and confrontation with public security forces \citep{arias2006drugs}. Militias grew out of networks of current and former police, firefighters, and military personnel. They extract rents from service monopolies in gas, internet, transit, and real estate, and they participate in elections through voter mobilization, campaign finance, and political protection networks \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. The prediction is that territorial control should compress electoral competition when armed governance is embedded in local state authority. The same effect should be weaker when armed governance is mainly confrontational.

We match annual georeferenced territory maps from Fogo Cruzado to polling-station returns from five municipal elections in the 20 municipalities of metropolitan Rio de Janeiro. Identification comes from staggered territorial expansion. As faction polygons grow across election cycles, previously untreated polling stations enter faction territory at different times. We estimate heterogeneity-robust average treatment effects \citep{callaway2021did} separately by faction type and compare newly treated stations with not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type. This comparison removes the fixed differences between inside-territory and outside-any-territory stations that would confound a never-treated design.

Militia territorial expansion reduces mayoral electoral competition on all three measures. Under the preferred not-yet-treated specification, vote concentration rises by 0.039, the effective number of candidates falls by 0.347, the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points, and turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. Relative to pre-treatment militia baselines, these estimates imply an 11 percent increase in concentration, a 10 percent decline in effective candidates, and a 25 percent wider mayoral margin. Drug-faction expansion produces no detectable effects on the same outcomes under the same design. Formal equality tests reject equality between militia and drug-faction effects for all three mayoral competition outcomes. Neither faction type affects open-list proportional city-council races, which is consistent with armed groups concentrating political resources where executive rents are largest and candidate coordination is easiest.

The paper contributes a polling-station-level estimate of how armed territorial control affects local elections. It uses Fogo Cruzado annual territory polygons for causal electoral analysis and compares militia and drug-faction expansion inside the same metropolitan economy. The contrast separates the political consequences of territorial control from the institutional form of criminal governance. The results also complement work on the elections-to-militia channel \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. That work shows how elected officials protect militia expansion. We document the reverse channel, in which militia territorial expansion compresses mayoral competition. The evidence points to a feedback loop in weak-state environments, where territorial control and electoral capture reinforce each other when armed groups are embedded in the state \citep{lessing2021, acemoglu2020weak}.

\section{Institutional Context}\label{sec:context}
%==========================================================================

Rio de Janeiro's armed factions emerged in the 1980s with the expansion of cocaine trafficking through the city's \emph{favelas} \citep{arias2006drugs}. The Comando Vermelho (CV) was the first major drug trafficking organization, followed by rivals Terceiro Comando Puro (TCP) and Amigos dos Amigos (ADA). These factions control territory through armed force, collecting ``taxes'' from residents and businesses and regulating daily life within their domains \citep{lessing2017logics}.

Militias\footnote{Paramilitary groups composed primarily of active and retired police, firefighters, and military personnel} expanded rapidly from the early 2000s, particularly in Rio's West Zone \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. Unlike drug factions, militias monopolize the provision of cooking gas, internet, public transit, and real estate within their territories. By 2024, militia-controlled territories covered a larger area than drug factions in the metropolitan region.

Criminal-governance theory clarifies the contrast between the two groups. \citet{magaloni2020killing} classify regimes by territorial monopoly, the relationship with the state, and treatment of the community. Rio's drug factions usually fit \emph{Insurgent} or \emph{Bandit} regimes, where armed groups confront state forces. Militias fit \emph{Symbiotic} or \emph{Predatory} regimes, where criminal rule is tied to collusion. \citet{blattman2023gangrule} make a related point. State rule and criminal rule can complement each other when armed groups hold rents worth protecting. Militias are an extreme case because the police-militia overlap brings criminal governance into the state's own personnel networks. \citet{monteiro2023enterprises} document the economic side of this contrast. Militias dominate local provision of cooking gas, cable TV and internet, water and electricity, and real estate. Drug factions remain more concentrated in drug retail. Broader economic stakes raise the value of local political control.

The expected electoral effect differs by faction type. Drug factions operate in a confrontational relationship with the state and have narrower economic footprints. This limits both their incentive and their institutional capacity to convert territorial control into vote shares. Militias operate through collusive relationships and hold rents that depend on local-state cooperation. They have stronger reasons to translate territory into electoral capture \citep{trejo2020votes, snyder2009protection, pantaleao2025elections}. Militia influence should grow as territory is held over successive election cycles. Drug-faction expansion should have a weaker effect on electoral competition. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} maps faction territories and polling stations in 2008 and 2024.

\section{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:data}
%==========================================================================

The analysis is at the polling-station-by-election-year level. We construct treatment from annual faction territory maps produced by the Fogo Cruzado Institute, a Rio civil-society observatory that georeferences control polygons for paramilitary militias and drug-trafficking organizations in metropolitan Rio de Janeiro \citep{fogocruzado2024}. The maps combine official gazettes, local press coverage, and triangulated incident reports on armed confrontation. Annual polygons cover 2007 to 2024. Appendix~\ref{app:measurement} documents treatment construction and validation. The measure captures the timing of \textit{de facto} territorial control as recorded by Fogo Cruzado. It is not an administrative or legal measure. Detection may be more systematic for militia territory, which often operates through visible service monopolies, than for drug territory, which is more fluid and covert.

Electoral competition outcomes are constructed from polling-station-level returns published by Brazil's Superior Electoral Tribunal \citep{tse2024} for five municipal elections in 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, and 2024. For each polling station, election, and office (\emph{prefeito} or \emph{vereador}), we compute the Herfindahl-Hirschman index of candidate vote shares (HHI), the effective number of candidates (ENC, following \citealt{laakso1979}), and the margin of victory between the first- and second-place candidates. We also obtain the number of eligible voters at each station-election from TSE, which we use as analytic weights and as a baseline demographic proxy.

The estimation sample is an unbalanced panel of 4,180 polling stations across five elections. Following \citet{monteiro2023enterprises}, we keep treated stations with a stable faction-type classification during the treated period. The sample contains 398 stations under militia control and 240 under drug-trafficking control. We exclude stations that switch between militia and drug control to isolate faction-specific mechanisms. The remaining 3,791 polling stations never fall inside a stable militia or drug-faction territory from 2007 to 2024 and form the never-treated pool. Treatment cohorts are defined by the first election year in which a station falls inside the faction's territory. Stations inside territory since the first map year in 2007 are assigned $g = 2008$. The staggered timing of entry provides the identifying variation.

The outcomes capture different margins of electoral competition. HHI measures the concentration of vote shares across candidates and rises when votes concentrate on one candidate. ENC, the Laakso-Taagepera effective number of candidates, expresses the same distribution in units of equivalent competing candidates and falls when competition declines. The margin of victory rises when the winner's lead widens. These outcomes help distinguish mechanisms. Coercive mobilization toward a preferred candidate should raise HHI, lower ENC, and widen the margin. Candidate deterrence should lower ENC and widen the margin, with a less direct effect on HHI. Effects that move all three measures in the same direction are consistent with both channels operating through militia control.

We exploit the staggered timing of faction territorial entry in a difference-in-differences framework. Let $G_i \in \{2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024, \infty\}$ denote the first election year at which polling station $i$ falls inside a faction territory of a given type, with $G_i = \infty$ for never-treated stations. Treatment is effectively irreversible within our sample given the stable-classification restriction. Once a station enters militia or drug territory under the Monteiro et al.\ classification, it remains attributed to that faction throughout.

The design estimates group-time average treatment effects on the treated,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:att_gt}
ATT(g,t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(g) - Y_t(\infty) \mid G_i = g\big],
\end{equation}
where $Y_t(g)$ is the potential outcome at election $t$ under territorial entry in cohort $g$, and $Y_t(\infty)$ is the potential outcome without treatment. We estimate $ATT(g,t)$ using the doubly robust estimator of \citet{callaway2021did}. The estimator combines inverse probability weighting with outcome regression and is consistent if either the propensity score or the outcome model is correctly specified. Observations are weighted by the number of eligible voters at each station-election, so estimated ATTs represent effects on the voter population rather than on the polling-station population. These populations differ in dense urban voting locations.

The identifying assumption is conditional parallel trends. Conditional on pre-treatment covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$, the expected change in untreated potential outcomes is the same for group-$g$ stations and the comparison group. Using not-yet-treated stations of the same faction type as controls,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cpt}
\mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(\infty) - Y_{t-1}(\infty) \mid \mathbf{X}_i,\, G_i = g\big]
  \;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\big[Y_t(\infty) - Y_{t-1}(\infty) \mid \mathbf{X}_i,\, G_i > t\big]
\end{equation}
for all $t \geq g$. In the main specification we set $\mathbf{X}_i = \emptyset$, imposing unconditional parallel trends within each faction-type pool. Conditioning is tightly constrained by the polling-station level of analysis. Stations are administrative units rather than survey units, and routinely observed station-level covariates are limited to the number of eligible voters used as weights, the parent municipality, and the station's own voting record.

Faction territorial entry may be endogenous to polling-station electoral dynamics. Armed groups may expand into neighborhoods where a politically aligned candidate is vulnerable, or where support for that candidate is already consolidating. The reverse-causality channel documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}, in which elected officials protect militia expansion, is a direct concern. A second concern is geographic contagion. Militia expansion often follows existing territorial boundaries that correlate with persistent features of the Rio metropolitan map, including the West Zone and the Baixada Fluminense. A third concern is the 2020 cohort, which overlaps with the COVID-19 shock to campaigns and turnout.

The rollout of Rio's Pacifying Police Units (UPPs) from 2008 to 2014 helps with the first concern. UPPs occupied 38 favelas and displaced incumbent drug factions. Their rollout followed logistical and political criteria tied to the 2014 World Cup and 2016 Olympics, rather than station-level electoral dynamics \citep{ferraz2023upp, magaloni2020killing}. The criminal-governance literature indicates that militias expanded into territorial vacuums created by drug-faction displacement from UPPs and later state crackdowns \citep{pantaleao2025elections, monteiro2023enterprises}. If the militia-entry cohorts from 2012 to 2020 partly reflect these state-induced displacement shocks, the timing is not driven only by local political conditions. The estimates should be read as effects of militia territorial entry conditional on this state-policy component of entry timing.

The within-faction-type NYT comparison restricts the counterfactual to stations drawn from the same institutional mechanism. This removes selection into faction type. It also helps with geographic contagion because treated and not-yet-treated stations come from the same expansion process. Event-study inspection addresses the COVID concern. If the 2020 cohort drove the aggregate effect, the path would show a discontinuity at $e=0$ driven by post-2020 cells. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows a more stable dynamic pattern.

The preferred specification uses not-yet-treated (NYT) controls of the same faction type. For a militia-treated station in cohort $g$, controls are militia-classified stations with $G_i > t$. The drug-faction design is analogous. These controls come from the same institutional mechanism as treated units, so the comparison isolates timing from faction-type selection. The alternative never-treated (NT) pool contains 3,791 stations outside stable militia and drug-faction territory. It is cleaner as an unexposed benchmark but differs from treated stations on preexisting political trajectories. NT and NYT identify different estimands under different parallel-trend assumptions. We use NYT in the main text and report NT estimates in Appendix~\ref{app:nt}.

Because treatment is defined on annual territory maps while elections occur every four years, the first post-treatment observation may reflect only partial within-cycle exposure for stations that entered territory late in the four-year cycle. If militia territory expanded to include a station in 2018, that station's first post-treatment election is 2020, with only two years of exposure. This feature tends to attenuate short-run effects. The observed ATT at $e = 0$ is a lower bound on the fully exposed effect.

For the dynamic analysis, event time is measured in elections relative to cohort entry. The universal base period is $e = -1$, so coefficients are deviations from the election immediately before treatment. We summarize dynamics using the aggregation in \citet{callaway2021did}. It averages $ATT(g,t)$ cells by event time $e$ and then averages the post-treatment path with weights proportional to the number of cohorts that reach each horizon. Figures show $e \in \{-12, -8, -4, 0, +4\}$ years relative to cohort entry. Standard errors are clustered at the polling-station level using the influence-function multiplier bootstrap in the \texttt{did} R package. Appendix~\ref{app:clustering} reports sensitivity to broader territory-polygon and municipality clusters. Pre-trends are assessed with a joint Wald test on all event-study coefficients for $e < 0$.

In mayoral races, pre-treatment militia stations have an average HHI of 0.350, an effective number of candidates of 3.43, a margin of victory of 0.21, and turnout of 0.52. Drug-treated and never-treated stations are broadly similar on these dimensions. In city-council races, open-list proportional representation produces a much less concentrated vote distribution. ENC averages around 30 candidates, while HHI and the margin of victory are much smaller than in mayoral races. These baselines calibrate the treatment effects below.

\subsection{Mayoral Competition}\label{sec:prefeito_results}

Militia territorial expansion reduces competition in mayoral races. Under the preferred NYT specification in Table~\ref{tab:csdid_main}, vote concentration rises by 0.039, the effective number of candidates falls by 0.347, the margin of victory widens by 5.2 percentage points, and turnout rises by 1.8 percentage points. All four estimates are significant at the one percent level and pass the joint Wald pre-trends test.\footnote{We also apply the relative-magnitude sensitivity framework of \citet{rambachan2023honest}. The three militia and \emph{prefeito} competition effects remain robust to plausible post-treatment deviations from parallel trends. Appendix~\ref{app:honestdid} reports the full sensitivity tables, robust confidence sets, and breakdown values $\overline{M}$.} The event-study paths in Figure~\ref{fig:es_main} show flat pre-treatment coefficients and post-treatment effects that appear at the treatment election and persist through the next cycle. Drug-faction expansion does not produce detectable changes in mayoral competition under the same design.

Appendix~\ref{app:mde} formalizes the militia-drug contrast. Equality tests reject equal effects for all three mayoral competition outcomes ($p = 0.011$ for HHI, $p = 0.008$ for ENC, and $p = 0.032$ for margin of victory). Power remains limited in the smaller drug-faction sample. At 80\% power, the design can detect drug-faction effects of 0.039 HHI points, 0.401 effective candidates, and 5.6 percentage points in the margin of victory. We read the drug estimates as evidence that the design does not detect comparable electoral compression for drug factions. They do not prove that drug factions have no electoral influence in any context.

The estimates are large relative to pre-treatment militia baselines. The HHI coefficient of $+$0.039 is about 11 percent of the baseline of 0.350. The ENC coefficient of $-$0.347 is about a 10 percent reduction relative to 3.43 effective candidates. The margin coefficient of $+$0.052 widens the baseline margin by about 25 percent. The turnout coefficient of $+$0.018 is a 3.4 percent increase relative to a baseline of 0.52. Militia entry shifts mayoral outcomes within one election cycle, and the dynamic estimates show persistence into the next cycle.

Turnout helps distinguish mobilization from suppression. Militia control could compress competition by bringing additional voters to the polls and directing them toward allied candidates. It could also work by keeping opposition supporters home while the militia-aligned base votes. Both channels can raise HHI and widen the margin, but they imply opposite turnout effects. The positive turnout estimate supports the mobilization interpretation. It also connects the result to work showing that exposure to armed-group violence can raise political participation through expressive or psychological channels \citep{blattman2009voting}.

\subsection{City-Council Competition}\label{sec:vereador_results}

City-council races show a different pattern. Under the preferred NYT specification in Table~\ref{tab:csdid_vereador}, none of the three competition measures moves significantly for either faction type. The militia estimates for HHI ($+0.001$), ENC ($-0.072$), and the margin of victory ($+0.003$) are small relative to both their baselines and the mayoral estimates. Relative to militia city-council baselines, they imply a 3.1 percent rise in HHI, a 0.2 percent decline in effective candidates, and a 3.9 percent wider margin. The corresponding mayoral effects are 11 percent, 10 percent, and 25 percent. Drug-faction estimates are also small and insignificant. The NYT pre-trends tests do not reject, so the null is not driven by an evident failure of the within-faction-type design.

%==========================================================================
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%==========================================================================

Militia territorial expansion compresses mayoral competition on all three measures. Relative to pre-treatment militia baselines, the estimates imply an 11 percent rise in HHI, a 10 percent reduction in effective candidates, and a 25 percent wider margin of victory. The turnout result speaks to the mechanism. Vote concentration rises together with participation, which points toward coerced mobilization for allied candidates rather than turnout suppression.

This pattern fits the institutional position of militias, which operate through entangled relationships with the state \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. Militias can deliver concentrated electoral support to allied politicians. In return, allied politicians can shield militia operations through bureaucratic appointments and law-enforcement priorities. The design captures this exchange as it accumulates over successive elections. Drug-faction expansion produces no detectable effects on any outcome, including turnout. That pattern is consistent with drug factions maintaining control through armed presence and confrontation with the state, with weaker channels of direct political engagement \citep{arias2006drugs, barnes2022territorial}. Appendix~\ref{app:mde} shows why the drug estimates should not be read as proof of zero influence.

The militia-drug contrast also matches the semi-strong-state framework in \citet{blattman2023gangrule}. A semi-strong state can police illicit markets through repression, crackdowns, and targeted occupations such as Rio's UPPs. It cannot fully dismantle criminal-governance infrastructure, especially when state personnel are embedded in that infrastructure. In this setting, state rule and criminal rule can be complements. Armed groups with rents to protect may respond to state presence by governing more. Rio militias are an extreme case because the police-militia overlap documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections} blurs the line between state rule and criminal rule at the local security level. Criminal rents can then become state-institutional capture through elections. Drug factions sit outside this equilibrium and have less access to the institutional channels needed to translate territory into votes.

Complementarity also helps explain why effects concentrate on mayoral races. Mayoral contests involve a single executive office with authority over municipal resources, security priorities, and land-use regulation \citep{ferraz2011electoral}. These domains matter for militia operations. \citet{monteiro2023enterprises} document that militias operate across cooking gas, cable TV, water, electricity, real estate, extortion, and drugs. This makes mayoral policy valuable. Open-list proportional \emph{vereador} races spread competition across many candidates and seats, which dilutes the effect of concentrated support at any single polling station. The same institutional logic that makes mayoral capture valuable also makes city-council effects harder to detect in these outcomes.

The scope condition is institutional rather than geographic. The mechanism should travel to settings where armed groups are embedded in state personnel, control rents that depend on local public authority, and can bargain with elected officials over protection, enforcement, and contracts. In those environments, territorial expansion gives armed groups the incentive and capacity to shape executive elections. The mechanism should be weaker for groups that rule through confrontation with the state, depend on narrower illicit-market rents, or lack a credible channel to exchange votes for state protection. The Rio militia-drug contrast does not imply that one Brazilian organization is uniquely political. It shows that criminal territorial control becomes electorally consequential when criminal governance and state authority operate as complements.

%==========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==========================================================================

Militia territorial expansion reduces mayoral competition in metropolitan Rio de Janeiro. Vote concentration rises, the effective number of candidates falls, and the margin of victory widens. These effects pass pre-trends diagnostics and remain robust to plausible violations of parallel trends. Drug-faction expansion produces no detectable effect under the same design. Estimates for open-list proportional \emph{vereador} races are also indistinguishable from zero under the preferred specification.

The asymmetry supports the institutional model of political influence documented by \citet{pantaleao2025elections}. Militias convert territorial control into electoral capital through voter mobilization and political protection networks that build over successive election cycles. Drug factions show no detectable temporal effects on electoral competition in this design. Interventions aimed only at the physical presence of armed groups may miss the institutional machinery that connects territorial control to political power.

\section{Treatment Measurement and Data Provenance}\label{app:measurement}
%==========================================================================

Fogo Cruzado territory maps classify areas of metropolitan Rio de Janeiro by observed armed-group control. They combine official gazettes, local press coverage, and triangulated incident reports on armed confrontation. We treat the maps as measures of observed \textit{de facto} territorial exposure, not as legal or administrative boundaries. This distinction matters because armed governance is more visible in some contexts than in others. Militia control often operates quasi-openly through service monopolies and territorial regulation. Drug-faction control can be more fluid and covert.

We ran three validation checks before assigning treatment status to polling stations. Adjacent annual map vintages show that 97.2\% of militia-classified polygons persist across consecutive years, consistent with the long-run stability of militia control documented in the institutional literature \citep{pantaleao2025elections}. We also cross-checked faction-type classifications against the stable classification of \citet{monteiro2023enterprises} and retained only stations whose attribution is consistent under the stable-classification rule. Finally, we inspected a random sample of polygons against the underlying incident logs and verified that the recorded incidents match the attributed armed-group type. The treatment measures exposure to observed territorial control under a documented mapping protocol. Residual measurement error is more likely to attenuate drug-faction effects than to generate the militia-prefeito pattern.

\section{Honest-DiD Sensitivity Analysis}\label{app:honestdid}

\section{Sample Construction}\label{app:sample}
%==========================================================================

Two features of the raw data complicate panel construction. TSE voting locations are administrative units that change across election cycles. Polling stations can open, close, move, or be renumbered between municipal elections. Fogo Cruzado faction polygons are also revised annually, so ever-controlled territory is larger than any single year's polygon set. A naive year-by-year intersection would mix real territorial entry with changes in administrative and cartographic records. We anchor the panel to time-invariant voting-location and territory frames.

\subsection{Master voting-location roster}

We start from the TSE voting-location file for each election in 2010 through 2024, the first year in which TSE began publishing geocoded latitude and longitude at the voting-location level.\footnote{TSE voting-location files before 2010 do not carry native coordinates. For the 2008 election we inherit coordinates from the earliest subsequent election in which the station appears, under the assumption that voting-location physical addresses are stable across two cycles unless the station was reconstituted.} For each election, we filter to the 20 metropolitan municipalities using the TSE municipality-code crosswalk, and we deduplicate within the election by the triple $(\text{CD\_MUNICIPIO}, \text{NR\_ZONA}, \text{NR\_LOCAL\_VOTACAO})$, which uniquely identifies a polling station within an election.

We build a master roster from the union of all distinct station triples observed in any election year. This gives 4,654 unique stations observed at some point from 2010 to 2024. For the 4,180 stations with coordinates in at least one year, the canonical location is the most recent valid $(\text{lat}, \text{lon})$ pair in the TSE files. The 474 stations without coordinates are dropped from the spatial analysis. The most-recent-coordinate rule avoids spurious station movement when TSE revises the recorded address of a stable physical location. Visual inspection suggests that these address revisions are the main source of year-to-year coordinate changes.

The master roster is unbalanced. Of the 4,180 geocoded stations, 1,740 (41.6\%) appear with a valid mayoral outcome in all five elections, 173 (4.1\%) in four, 1,024 (24.5\%) in three, 979 (23.4\%) in two, 217 (5.2\%) in one, and 47 (1.1\%) in none. The concentration in the three-election and two-election rows reflects a major TSE redistricting of voting locations between 2016 and 2020. Many legacy stations appear only in the elections from 2008 to 2016, while many new stations enter in 2020. We use the unbalanced-panel option in the \texttt{did} R package, which uses each station's available observations to identify cohort-specific $ATT(g,t)$ cells. This keeps the full sample but creates uneven support across cohort cells. The event-study plots show that no single cohort or horizon is identified from a thin subset.

\subsection{Annual master territory map}

Fogo Cruzado polygons are published annually and revised as territorial disputes unfold. A station inside a drug-faction polygon in 2010 may be reclassified as militia-controlled in 2018 after a takeover. It may also leave the controlled-polygon set in a year when Fogo Cruzado records a reduction in active armed-group presence. Intersecting each election's voting locations with only that year's polygon map would define treatment against a shifting baseline and mix territorial entry with cartographic revision.

We construct an annual master territory map as the union of all 18 annual control polygons published between 2007 and 2024, computed in UTM 23S projection (EPSG:31983) for metric accuracy. Each voting location receives two measures. The year-specific inside-polygon indicator builds the treatment cohort $G_i$. For each station-year pair, we record whether the station falls inside that year's control polygon and the dominant faction type. The second measure is distance to the ever-controlled envelope, a time-invariant geographic covariate used in robustness checks.

The master map reduces annual polygon noise. Minor boundary revisions between consecutive vintages are subsumed into the union and do not generate spurious entries and exits. Transient gaps, where a station falls inside one map but outside the adjacent vintages, are flagged for manual inspection rather than treated as reversals. In our sample, these cases trace to incident-reporting gaps rather than substantive territorial withdrawal. Master-map distance also gives a stable geographic reference for robustness checks that should not mechanically follow Fogo Cruzado's year-to-year revisions.

Switchers are likely selected on the geographic and political conditions that make an area contested. Dropping them narrows the scope of inference to stable faction control but sharpens identification. A robustness specification that treats switchers as a third treatment arm is feasible but outside the current analysis. The final sample requires a valid mayoral outcome in at least one pre-treatment and one post-treatment period, a stable faction-type classification, valid coordinates, and location in one of the 20 metropolitan municipalities. These restrictions produce the 398 stable-militia and 240 stable-drug treated stations, plus the 3,791 never-treated stations reported in the main text and displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:maps}.
